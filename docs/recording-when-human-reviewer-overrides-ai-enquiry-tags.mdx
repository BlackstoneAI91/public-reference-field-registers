## Why a correction register belongs beside an AI enquiry pilot

An enquiry arrives with a short message and a few details. Someone, or something, decides which category it belongs to: a quote request, a support question, a partnership note, a billing matter. When a person makes that call, the reasoning usually stays in their head. When an AI system makes it, the reasoning is invisible unless the team writes it down.

That is the gap this register fills. It is not a performance report and it is not a scorecard for the tool. It is an administrative record of the moments when a human reviewer looked at an AI-assigned enquiry tag and decided it was wrong, unclear, or close enough to need a second opinion. The register captures what the tag was, what it became, why the reviewer changed it, and when the change should be looked at again.

The example throughout this guide is fictional: a small Malaysian service business running a limited pilot in which AI-generated enquiry categories sit alongside human review. No real customer messages, names or contact details appear here, and none should appear in your own register. The register works on labels and reasons, not on the content of what people wrote.

This is administrative housekeeping. It does not tell you whether an AI system is accurate, and it should not be used to make promises about accuracy to anyone. Its value is that it makes review decisions visible, repeatable and easy to hand over when the person doing the reviewing changes. Teams that treat AI as part of a wider operating system, rather than a standalone novelty, tend to need this kind of record early. That is the thinking an AI agency usually brings to a pilot: not just the model, but the review loop around it.

## What the register records, and what it deliberately leaves out

A useful register has a narrow scope. Each row is one correction event, not one enquiry. If a reviewer changes two tags on the same enquiry, that is two rows, because the reasons may differ.

Columns that earn their place:

Row ID. A simple sequential reference, such as R-001, so a row can be discussed without quoting the enquiry.

Review date. The date the human looked at the tag, in a consistent format. Use one format across the whole sheet; mixing day-first and month-first dates is the most common way these registers become unreadable after a few months.

Original tag. The category the AI output proposed, copied exactly as it appeared.

Corrected tag. The category the reviewer chose instead, or the word Unchanged if the tag was accepted but the reasoning was worth noting.

Reason code. A short controlled label for why the change happened, drawn from a small fixed list.

Reviewer note. One or two sentences in plain language. No customer text, no names, no contact details.

Next review date. When this row should be revisited, usually to check whether the same reason keeps appearing.

Status. Open, resolved, or parked, so the sheet does not fill with rows nobody intends to revisit.

Columns that usually cause trouble: free-text copies of the original message, customer identifiers, reviewer personal opinions about the tool, and any field that invites a guess about why the AI behaved as it did. The register records the human decision and its stated reason. Speculation about internal model behaviour belongs nowhere in an administrative log.

If your organisation already keeps a brand knowledge profile with source-linked facts, the same discipline applies: record what was observed and who observed it, and leave the interpretation for a separate discussion.

Category labels used in the pilot:

Sample rows, all fictional:

R-001. Review date 2026-03-04. Original tag: Partnership or supplier. Corrected tag: Quote request. Reason code: Wrong intent. Reviewer note: The message described a one-off purchase enquiry rather than an ongoing supply arrangement. Next review: 2026-04-04. Status: Open.

R-002. Review date 2026-03-04. Original tag: Service question. Corrected tag: Existing booking. Reason code: Missing context. Reviewer note: The sender referred to a prior arrangement that the tag did not reflect. Next review: 2026-04-04. Status: Open.

R-003. Review date 2026-03-11. Original tag: Quote request. Corrected tag: Quote request. Reason code: Accepted with note. Reviewer note: Tag kept, but the enquiry combined two services and may need splitting at intake. Next review: 2026-05-11. Status: Parked.

R-004. Review date 2026-03-18. Original tag: Careers. Corrected tag: Unclear or other. Reason code: Ambiguous wording. Reviewer note: The message could be read as a general introduction rather than a job enquiry. Next review: 2026-04-18. Status: Open.

R-005. Review date 2026-03-25. Original tag: Billing or payment. Corrected tag: Service question. Reason code: Wrong intent. Reviewer note: The sender asked how a charge is calculated, which the team treats as a service question. Next review: 2026-04-25. Status: Resolved after a second reviewer agreed.

Notice what the rows do not contain: no message text, no sender name, no email address, no phone number. A reader can follow the decision without ever seeing the enquiry.

## Reason codes: a small list beats a long one

The reason code is the part of the register that turns a pile of rows into something a team can actually discuss. Keep the list short and closed. If reviewers can type anything, the codes drift within weeks and stop being comparable.

A workable starter list for a fictional pilot:

Wrong intent. The tag described a different kind of enquiry from the one the reviewer judged it to be.

Missing context. The tag was plausible but incomplete, usually because the enquiry referred to something earlier.

Ambiguous wording. The message could reasonably sit in more than one category.

Label mismatch. The tag was close but used a category name the team does not use.

Accepted with note. The tag was kept, but the reviewer flagged something worth watching.

Escalated. The reviewer was not confident and passed the row to a second person.

Two rules keep the codes honest. First, the reviewer writes the reason before seeing anyone else's view, so the register records independent judgement rather than consensus. Second, a code is only added when at least two rows need it; one-off codes belong in the note, not the list.

This is where a register differs from a satisfaction survey. It is not asking whether people liked the outcome. It is recording a decision and its stated basis, which is the same principle behind evidence-led review: a result should come with supporting evidence and a recommended correction, not an unexplained verdict.

## Dates, cadence and the review meeting

Dates do most of the work in a register. Without them, rows accumulate and nobody returns to them.

A simple cadence, offered as an example rather than a rule: review new rows weekly, revisit open rows monthly, and re-read parked rows quarterly. Adjust to the volume your pilot actually produces; a business receiving a handful of enquiries a week does not need a daily ritual.

At each review meeting, three questions are enough:

Which reason code appeared most often since the last meeting?

Are there rows where two reviewers disagreed, and what does that tell us about the label definitions rather than the tool?

Which open rows can now be closed, and which need a decision from someone with authority to change the category list?

The next review date on each row should be a real commitment, not a placeholder. If a row is parked, say why in the note. If a row is resolved, record who confirmed it and on what date, so a later reader does not reopen a settled question.

Keep the register in one place with a clear owner. A shared spreadsheet is fine for a pilot; the important thing is that there is one copy, one owner and one date format. When ownership changes, the new owner should be able to read the last three months of rows and understand the pattern without asking anyone.

## Separating the register from the system it reviews

A correction register is only useful if it stays independent of the thing it is reviewing. The moment the register becomes a place to argue for or against a particular tool, the reasons stop being honest.

Three practical separations help:

Separate the register from the enquiry inbox. The register references a row ID, not a message. If someone needs to check the original, they do it in the inbox under whatever access rules already apply, not by pasting content into the log.

Separate observation from interpretation. The note says what the reviewer decided and why. It does not say the system is unreliable, or that it is improving. Those are conclusions for a later discussion with evidence behind them.

Separate the pilot from the public claim. Nothing in the register should be turned into a statement about accuracy, speed or outcomes on a website or in a proposal. A register is an internal administrative record.

This separation matters most when a business is working with an outside partner. ## A copyable starting template

The block below is plain text so it can be pasted into a spreadsheet or document and adapted. It is a template, not a finished policy.

Register name: AI Enquiry Tag Correction Register (Pilot)
Owner: [role, not a personal name]
Date format: YYYY-MM-DD
Review cadence: weekly new rows, monthly open rows, quarterly parked rows

Columns:
Row ID | Review date | Original tag | Corrected tag | Reason code | Reviewer note | Next review date | Status

Reason codes:
Wrong intent | Missing context | Ambiguous wording | Label mismatch | Accepted with note | Escalated

Status values:
Open | Resolved | Parked

Rules:
No customer message text, names, contact details or identifiers in any field.
One row per correction event.
Reason code chosen from the fixed list; anything else goes in the note.
Next review date is a commitment, not a placeholder.
Resolved rows record who confirmed and when.

Sample row:
R-006 | 2026-04-01 | Service question | Quote request | Wrong intent | Enquiry described a specific job rather than a general question. | 2026-05-01 | Open

Adapt the labels to your own categories, but keep the shape. A short register is easier to hand over when staff change.
