## What a route check actually tests

A Kuala Lumpur campaign can look ready while the enquiry route behind it is still untested. The landing page loads, the form submits, and the confirmation message appears. None of that proves the enquiry reached a person who could act on it, or that the person who received it knew which branch should reply.

A route check is a rehearsal of the whole path, not a review of one page. It starts wherever a visitor first arrives, follows the handover into a staffed destination, and ends with a written record of what happened. The point is to find the gap between the page and the person before the campaign spends money sending strangers into it.

Three things are often confused during preparation:

Starting pages. The paid landing page, the organic service page, and the location page are entry points. They are not the destination.
The handover. The form submission, phone call, WhatsApp message or booking request that moves the enquiry from the website to a staffed queue.
The destination. The named person or team who reads the enquiry, decides who owns it, and replies within an agreed time.

A campaign can be well designed and still fail this test if the starting pages point at a destination that is unstaffed, out of market, or unclear about which branch should respond. The check is deliberately boring. It produces evidence, not opinions.

## Separate the starting page from the staffed destination

The most common structural mistake is treating the landing page as if it were the destination. A Kuala Lumpur campaign page may be built to attract interest, but interest has to land somewhere with a name attached.

Write the route as a short chain before you test anything:

Entry: which page or pages a campaign visitor can reach first.
Capture: what the visitor does to make contact, and what information the page asks for.
Handover: how that contact leaves the website and where it appears next.
Owner: the named person or team responsible for the first reply.
Fallback: what happens if the owner is unavailable, on leave, or the enquiry arrives outside working hours.

Each link in the chain needs a named owner. "The sales team" is not an owner. "The KL branch inbox, monitored by the branch coordinator during office hours, with the shared inbox as fallback" is an owner.

This is also where paid and organic routes diverge. A paid landing page may be built for a single offer and a single call to action. An organic service page may serve several intents and offer a phone number, a form and a location reference. Both can be correct, but they should not hand over to different destinations unless that difference is deliberate and documented.

If the campaign depends on a new or rebuilt page, the page brief should state the destination explicitly. A web design KL brief that stops at layout and copy leaves the handover to chance. The brief should name the receiving queue, the expected reply window, and the person who signs off that the queue is live.

## A fixture table for a hypothetical business with a KL branch

The table below is fictional. It exists to show the level of detail a route check needs, not to describe any real company, customer or result.

Assume a Malaysian service business headquartered outside Kuala Lumpur with a small KL branch. The branch handles on-site work in the Klang Valley. Head office handles enquiries from other states. The campaign targets Kuala Lumpur searches.

Route | Starting page | Capture method | Destination | Named owner | Fallback
A | Paid KL landing page | Short form with service and postcode | KL branch inbox | Branch coordinator | Shared inbox, next working day
B | Organic service page | Form plus phone number | Head office inbox | Service desk lead | Branch coordinator if postcode is in Klang Valley
C | Location page for the KL branch | Form only | KL branch inbox | Branch coordinator | Shared inbox
D | Out-of-market request (Johor) arriving on the KL page | Same KL form | Should route to head office | Service desk lead | Branch coordinator informs enquirer

Row D is the one that usually exposes a problem. A visitor from Johor who reaches a Kuala Lumpur page is not a failure of the campaign; it is a normal consequence of broad search behaviour. The question is whether the route recognises the postcode and sends the enquiry to the team that can actually serve it. If the KL form sends every enquiry to the branch, the branch will either attempt work outside its area or forward it manually, and neither outcome is documented.

Decide in advance how out-of-market requests are handled. Options include a postcode rule in the form, a routing note in the confirmation message, or a manual triage step with a stated time limit. Whichever you choose, write it down and test it with a fictional out-of-market submission before the campaign starts.

## Document page owners, reporting and arrival tracking

A route check produces a document, not a memory. Keep it short enough that someone will actually update it.

For each starting page, record:

Page owner: the person who approves changes to the page.
Route owner: the person accountable for the handover working.
Destination: the inbox, queue or booking system that receives the enquiry.
Reply expectation: an internal target for first response, agreed by the destination owner.
Reporting: where enquiries are counted, and who reviews the count.
Review date: when the route will next be tested.

Reporting deserves its own line because a campaign can generate enquiries that are never counted. If the form sends an email to one inbox and the campaign report counts form views, the two numbers describe different things. Agree which number the business will use to judge the campaign, and make sure the destination owner can see it.

Arrival tracking is the practical test. Submit a fictional enquiry through each route and confirm four things: it arrived, it arrived in the right place, it carried enough detail to act on, and the person who received it knew what to do next. Repeat for the phone number and any messaging channel the page displays. A route that works only for the person who built it is not yet a route.

## Worked example: a campaign paused by one unanswered route

Consider a fictional service business with a two-week campaign for its Kuala Lumpur branch. The branch page and the paid landing page look similar, but their forms go to different inboxes. In the rehearsal, a tester uses the paid route to request a callback. The site confirms receipt and the report records a conversion, yet the branch desk finds no request.

The campaign owner pauses the paid route while the page owner points the form at the monitored queue. The branch desk repeats the test on a phone, receives the request, and confirms who will respond after hours. Record the failed test, the change, and the second result before resuming the campaign.

## Checks to run when the campaign ends

The end of a campaign is the right moment to test the route again, because the conditions that made it work may have changed.

Run these checks in order:

Confirm the destination still exists. Inboxes get renamed, staff move, and shared queues are retired. Verify the receiving address or system is still monitored.
Confirm the owners are current. Update the route document with any change of branch coordinator, service desk lead or page owner.
Reconcile the counts. Compare the enquiries the campaign reported with the enquiries the destination actually received. Investigate any gap rather than averaging it away.
Review out-of-market requests. Count how many arrived and where they went. If the routing rule was manual, check whether it was applied consistently.
Retire or redirect campaign pages. A landing page left live after a campaign can keep collecting enquiries with no owner. Decide whether it stays, redirects, or is taken down.
Record what to change next time. One or two lines are enough.

## A short pre-launch checklist

Use this as a working checklist, not a score. The items are author heuristics drawn from the route method above; they are not research findings and do not predict campaign performance.

Route written down, with entry, capture, handover, owner and fallback for each starting page.
Every starting page has a named page owner and a named route owner.
Paid and organic starting pages are listed separately, with any difference in destination explained.
A fictional enquiry has been submitted through every route and confirmed as received.
The phone number and any messaging channel have been tested, not just the form.
An out-of-market request has been tested and its handling is documented.
The destination owner knows the reply expectation and the fallback.
Reporting is agreed: which number the business will use to judge the campaign.
Leave and availability are checked for the campaign period.
End-of-campaign checks are scheduled, including reconciliation of counts and retirement of campaign pages.

If a page needs rebuilding to support the route, the brief should carry the destination details, not just the design.
