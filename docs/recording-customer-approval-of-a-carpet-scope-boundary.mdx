## Why a rug needs its own line in the enquiry record

A fixed carpet belongs to a room. A rug belongs to whoever is using the room this month. That single difference is enough to break an otherwise tidy cleaning enquiry, because the room named in the first message may not be the room the rug occupies when the work is confirmed.

This guide sets out a scope register for that situation. It is a record-keeping tool, not treatment advice. It does not tell you how a rug should be cleaned, what it should cost, or what any particular fabric will tolerate. It tells you where to write down four things: the room listed at approval, the room the venue currently lists, the decision still pending, and who owns that decision.

The register suits Sarawak households with one good rug that travels between living and guest areas, accommodation operators who move rugs between units, and venue managers whose side rooms change function with each booking. ## The four columns that do the work

Keep the register to four working columns and one reference column. More columns invite people to write opinions instead of facts.

Column one, Item reference. A short label the venue controls, such as Rug A or the blue hall runner. Avoid room names here. The whole point is that the item outlives its location.

Column two, Room listed at approval. The room written into the quotation, written confirmation or booking note. Copy it exactly as it appears, including any wording like side room or front hall. If the approval names no room, write not stated rather than guessing.

Column three, Room currently listed by venue. What the venue says today. Date this entry. A room name without a date is a rumour.

Column four, Pending decision. One sentence describing what is unresolved, phrased as a question with an owner. For example: does the approved scope follow the rug to the front hall, or does the front hall need a separate line?

Column five, Reference. The message, quotation or confirmation the row is based on. This is what makes the register auditable when a coordinator changes.

Two rules keep the register honest. Never overwrite column two; add a new row instead. And never let column four sit blank when columns two and three disagree, because a blank pending decision reads as agreement.

## A complete worked example, from discovery to written clarification

The following example is invented for illustration. It describes no real premises and no real booking.

Discovery. A venue coordinator messages a cleaning provider about a rug in the side room. The provider quotes for a single small rug. The written confirmation records the item as Rug A, room listed at approval: side room. The register opens with one row.

Change. Three weeks later the venue moves Rug A to the front hall for an event and leaves it there. The coordinator updates column three: front hall, dated. Column two still reads side room. The register now shows a disagreement, and column four is filled: does the approved scope follow Rug A to the front hall, or does the front hall need its own line and its own confirmation?

Pending. Nobody answers immediately. The register keeps the question visible with an owner and a date raised. This is the stage where most confusion is created, not by the move itself but by the silence after it.

Clarification. The coordinator writes to the provider, quoting the item reference and both room names, and asks which scope applies. The provider replies in writing. The register gains a second row recording the answer and its reference. The first row stays untouched.

Close-out. The venue marks the pending decision resolved and notes the date. If the rug moves again, the cycle restarts with a new row rather than an edit.

Notice what the register never does. It does not decide the answer. It records that a question was asked, who asked it, and what came back.

## Writing the clarification message so it can be filed

A clarification message is only useful if it can be pasted into the reference column later. Write it so it stands alone.

Name the item reference first. Then state both rooms with their dates. Then ask one question. Then say what you will do while waiting.

An example structure, invented for illustration: We are writing about Rug A. Our approval records the side room. The rug has been in the front hall since the fourteenth. Please confirm whether the approved scope follows the rug to the front hall, or whether the front hall needs a separate line. Until we hear back, we are treating the front hall as unconfirmed.

That last sentence matters. It prevents a later reader from assuming the move was silently accepted. It also gives the provider a clear opening to correct the record rather than argue with it.

Keep the message free of treatment requests. Asking whether a scope follows an item is a records question. Asking how a stain should be handled is a different conversation, and mixing the two makes the reply harder to file.

When the reply arrives, file it against the item reference, not the room. Rooms change; the reference should not.

## A short checklist before you send the enquiry

The following is an author heuristic, not a standard or a researched finding. Treat the scores as prompts for review.

One point each: the item has a reference that is not a room name. The approval room is copied exactly, or marked not stated. The current room carries a date. The pending decision is written as a question with an owner. The reference column names a document or message. The clarification message names the item first and asks one question. The register shows what is being assumed while waiting.

Seven points is a complete row. Fewer than five suggests the row will not survive a change of coordinator.

Two habits are worth building alongside the checklist. First, review the register whenever a room changes function, not only when a booking is made. Second, keep the register in one place that the venue controls, so that a question about a rug does not depend on one person's inbox.

If the venue also sends curtains, gowns, shoes or soft toys for cleaning, give each category its own item reference in the same register. ## What the register deliberately leaves out

A scope register is more useful when it refuses certain content.

It leaves out treatment opinions. A note that a rug should be handled a particular way belongs in the provider's written confirmation, not in the venue's tracking sheet, where it can be mistaken for an instruction.

It leaves out prices as a running tally. Prices belong to quotations. A register that accumulates figures invites people to treat an old number as current.

It leaves out unnamed verbal agreements. If a decision was made by phone, the register records that a call happened and that written confirmation is awaited. It does not record the decision as settled.

It leaves out room inventories. A list of every room in the building is a different document with a different purpose. The register holds only items that have an open or recently closed scope question.

Finally, it leaves out blame. When a rug moves and nobody updates the record, the register should show the gap plainly, with dates, so the next person can close it. A register that reads as an accusation gets abandoned; one that reads as a log gets maintained.
