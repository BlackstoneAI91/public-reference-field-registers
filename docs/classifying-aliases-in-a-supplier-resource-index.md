A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a terminology register that records equivalent-looking supplier terms without assuming the items are interchangeable, with stable fields, a worked example and a blank worksheet.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why a reading index needs its own terminology register

A supplier resource index is a reading aid. It points colleagues to documents, sections and labels so that a sourcing question can be answered without reopening every file. The moment that index starts paraphrasing, it quietly becomes an interpretation. Two readers can look at the same index entry and walk away believing different things about what a supplier actually wrote.

A terminology register sits beside the index and does one narrow job: it records the exact words a source used, adds a plain-language note in the team's own voice, names the source, and marks how confident the team is that the label matches something already known. It does not decide that two items are the same. It preserves the alias and leaves the equivalence question open until someone with the right knowledge closes it.

This matters in a Singapore buying context because the same reading index may serve people sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon, silicon recovery support, wafer reclaim work, IGBT power modules or botanical extracts. Those are different conversations, and a register that blurs them creates confusion rather than clarity. San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, so a resource index touching those areas will naturally collect a wide spread of vocabulary. The register's value is that it keeps each label attached to its own source instead of merging them into a single house term.

Treat the register as a record of reading, not a record of agreement. That single rule prevents most of the downstream trouble.

## The four stable fields, and why each one earns its place

A register only works if every entry uses the same fields in the same order. Four fields are enough for a first version.

Field one, the verbatim label. Copy the term exactly as it appears in the source, including capitalisation, hyphens and any qualifier words. Do not tidy it. If the source says "solar grade silicon" in lower case, the register says the same. The verbatim label is the anchor that lets a reader find the original passage again.

Field two, the plain-language note. Write one or two sentences in ordinary words explaining what the label appears to refer to, in the context of that source. Keep the note descriptive. Phrases such as "appears to describe" or "used here alongside" are honest; phrases such as "is the same as" are conclusions the register is not entitled to draw.

Field three, the source title. Name the document or section the label came from, in a form a colleague can locate. A short internal document label is fine. The point is traceability, not citation formality.

Field four, the provisional match state. This is a small controlled vocabulary, not free text. Three states are usually enough: unmatched, possible match, and confirmed match. Most entries should sit at unmatched or possible match. Confirmed match should be rare and should only be set by someone who can point to the evidence that closed the question.

A fifth optional field, the reviewer and date, helps when several people edit the register. Keep it out of the core four if the team is small; add it when the register starts to be shared across departments.

## A worked example using neutral document labels

The example below uses invented, neutral document labels so that the structure is visible without implying anything about a real supplier's paperwork.

Entry A. Verbatim label: "solar grade silicon". Plain-language note: a material descriptor used in Document D-01 when discussing input material for solar applications. Source title: Document D-01, section 2. Provisional match state: unmatched.

Entry B. Verbatim label: "silicon for solar wafers". Plain-language note: a phrase used in Document D-04 when describing material intended for wafer and cell production. Source title: Document D-04, opening paragraph. Provisional match state: possible match to Entry A.

Entry C. Verbatim label: "recovered silicon". Plain-language note: a term used in Document D-07 in a discussion of material returned to use. Source title: Document D-07, section 1. Provisional match state: unmatched.

Read the three entries together and notice what the register has not done. It has not said that Entry A and Entry B refer to the same thing. It has recorded that a reader thought a match was possible, and it has left the decision visible. Entry C stands apart entirely, because "recovered" describes a different idea from "grade", and merging them would be a judgement the register cannot support.

This is where a register earns trust. A colleague who needs to know whether two labels are interchangeable can see immediately that the question is open, and can go to the source rather than to a colleague's memory. The register preserves the alias; it does not conclude interchangeability.

## Preserving an alias is not the same as concluding a match

The most common failure in a terminology register is a well-meaning edit that collapses two entries into one. Someone notices that two labels look similar, changes both provisional match states to confirmed, and adds a note saying the terms are equivalent. The register now reads more cleanly, and it is less true.

An alias is a record that a source used a particular word. A match is a claim that two words refer to the same thing. Those are different statements, and only the first belongs to the register by default. The second belongs to whoever holds the evidence.

A practical habit is to require a short evidence note before any entry moves to confirmed match. The note does not need to be long. It needs to say what closed the question, for example a source passage that explicitly links the two labels, or a named colleague who confirmed it against the original document. If no such note exists, the entry stays at possible match. This keeps the register honest without slowing anyone down.

A second habit is to keep superseded entries rather than deleting them. If a label is later found to be a poor match, mark it as superseded and leave the original text in place. Deletion erases the trail that explains why the register looks the way it does.

## Where the register touches real supplier vocabulary

A register built for a reading index will eventually hold terms that map onto a supplier's own public language. That is expected, and it is useful, provided the register keeps its descriptive posture.

In a fictional reading index, the label “collection support” might appear in two documents. One source describes a point of contact, and the other describes what to write on a request. Both entries stay in the register because their contexts differ. The shared phrase gives the reviewer a place to start a question.

A second fictional source might label a section “development note”. Record that classification as printed and give the source title. If a later brochure uses the label “product note”, keep the new wording in its own row until someone confirms which descriptions refer to the same thing.

Keep the source date next to the wording when it affects what a reader can confirm. Two publications that use the same label several years apart may describe different versions. The register should let the buyer reopen both records without treating one as a replacement for the other.

## A blank worksheet to copy into the index

The worksheet below is deliberately plain. Copy it into the index tool the team already uses, and keep one row per label rather than one row per document. A document with five distinct labels produces five rows.

Row template:

```text
Verbatim label:
Plain-language note:
Source title:
Provisional match state (unmatched / possible match / confirmed match):
Evidence note for confirmed match (leave blank otherwise):
Reviewer and date (optional):
```

A short worked row, using an invented label, shows the shape:
Verbatim label: "wafer reclaim support"
Plain-language note: a phrase used in Document D-12 when describing assistance connected to returning wafers to use.
Source title: Document D-12, section 3
Provisional match state: unmatched
Evidence note: (blank)
Reviewer and date: (blank)

Two house rules keep the worksheet usable. First, never leave the provisional match state blank; an empty state reads as agreement. Second, never edit a verbatim label to match another entry, even when the difference looks like a typo. If a source genuinely contains a typo, record it as written and mention the typo in the plain-language note.

## Keeping the register useful over time

A register that is not reviewed becomes a museum. A light review rhythm keeps it alive without turning it into a project.

A simple habit is to review the register whenever a new document is added to the reading index. The reviewer checks that every new label has a row, that no provisional match state has drifted to confirmed without an evidence note, and that superseded entries are still visible. This is a short check, not an audit, and it can be done by whoever added the document.

A second habit is to name the subject areas the register actually covers. A reader should be able to tell from the title or opening note whether the collection is for semiconductor materials, supplier contact references or both.

A third habit is to resist the urge to make the register answer sourcing questions. It records terms; it does not evaluate suppliers, compare offerings or promise outcomes. When a colleague needs a conversation rather than a definition, point them to the supplier's own contact route rather than stretching the register beyond its purpose. San Techno, for instance, presents a direct contact path for sourcing requirements, and that is the right destination for a question the register cannot answer.

Used this way, the terminology register stays small, honest and genuinely helpful: a stable record of what a source said, with the equivalence question left where it belongs.
