## Why a diagram needs three layers of words

A process diagram on a service page usually shows a sequence: enquiry arrives, details are checked, a quote is prepared, work is scheduled. The picture carries the shape of that sequence, but it cannot carry the reasoning. That is what the words around it are for.

There are three layers, and each does a different job. The caption sits with the image and tells every reader what they are looking at, including people who never load the image. The text alternative describes the diagram for someone using a screen reader or viewing the page with images switched off. The nearby copy is the ordinary paragraph before or after the diagram that explains why the sequence matters to the reader.

When these three layers repeat each other word for word, the page becomes longer without becoming clearer. When they are written as if they were one layer, something gets lost: a caption that only says "Our process" tells nobody anything, and a text alternative that dumps the entire surrounding paragraph into an attribute is exhausting to listen to.

A useful rule of thumb is that each layer should add something the others do not. The caption names the diagram and its scope. The text alternative states the sequence and any distinction the picture makes visually. The nearby copy explains the consequence for the reader. This article works through a fictional example, then offers a review brief you can hand to a web design services team and a plain-language check you can run yourself.

## A fictional worked example: the same diagram, three drafts

Imagine a Malaysian service company that installs and maintains office equipment. Its service page already has a paragraph explaining that a site visit is arranged after an enquiry, and that the visit confirms what equipment is needed. The page then shows a four-step diagram: Enquiry, Site Visit, Proposal, Installation.

Draft one, the common starting point. Caption: "Our process." Text alternative: "Process diagram." Nearby copy: a repeat of the paragraph above the image. Nothing here is wrong, but a reader who cannot see the diagram learns nothing from it, and a reader who can see it learns nothing new from the words.

Draft two, over-corrected. Caption: "Figure 1: Four-stage customer engagement workflow illustrating enquiry intake, site assessment, commercial proposal generation and equipment installation." Text alternative: the same sentence, plus the full paragraph, plus the company's opening hours. This is accurate but heavy. The caption reads like an internal document heading, and the text alternative has become a second article.

Draft three, layered. Caption: "How an installation request moves from enquiry to completed setup." Text alternative: "Four steps in order: enquiry received, site visit to confirm requirements, written proposal, installation scheduled after acceptance." Nearby copy, placed after the diagram: "The site visit is the step that decides the rest. If the equipment needed differs from what was described in the enquiry, the proposal is revised before any installation date is offered."

The third draft works because the diagram adds one distinction the surrounding copy did not make: the site visit can change the proposal. The caption frames the diagram, the text alternative carries the sequence, and the nearby copy explains the consequence. Notice that no layer claims a result, a timeline or a guarantee. It describes a process, which is all the diagram can honestly support.

## Writing the caption: name the diagram, not the company

A caption's job is orientation. A reader scrolling quickly should be able to tell, from the caption alone, what the diagram shows and whether it is worth their attention.

Keep it to one sentence. Lead with what the diagram depicts, not with the company name, because the reader already knows whose page they are on. "How a maintenance request is logged and assigned" is more useful than "Blackstone Intelligence maintenance workflow." If the diagram covers only part of a process, say so: "The first three steps of a five-step onboarding sequence." That prevents a reader from assuming the picture is complete.

Avoid figure numbering unless the page is a formal document with several referenced figures. On a service page, "Figure 2" signals a report, not a helpful illustration.

Do not use the caption to make a claim the diagram cannot show. A caption reading "Our faster process" asserts a comparison the picture does not evidence. "Where a request is checked before scheduling" describes what is actually visible.

Finally, check that the caption still makes sense if the image fails to load. If the caption alone leaves a reader confused about what is missing, rewrite it.

## Writing the text alternative: describe the sequence and the distinction

The text alternative is read aloud by screen readers and shown when images are blocked. It should convey the information the diagram carries, not its visual style. Nobody needs to hear about arrow colours.

Start with the structure: how many steps, and in what order. Then state any branch, loop or exception the diagram shows, because that is usually the part a reader cannot infer from the surrounding prose. If the diagram shows a step that can send a request back to an earlier stage, say so explicitly.

Keep it proportionate. A four-step diagram rarely needs more than two or three sentences. If your text alternative is longer than the nearby paragraph, the diagram is probably trying to carry too much, and the better fix may be to simplify the diagram rather than to describe it at length.

Do not paste the nearby copy into the text alternative. Duplication makes the page repetitive for screen reader users, who hear the same content twice. Do not write "image of" or "diagram showing" as a substitute for describing anything; those phrases add words without adding information.

If the diagram is purely decorative, such as a faint background pattern, it should have an empty text alternative so it is skipped. That is a different case from an informative process diagram, which always needs a real description.

## Writing the nearby copy: explain the consequence

The paragraph around the diagram is where most of the reader value sits, and it is the layer most often wasted on repetition.

Ask what the diagram changes for the reader. If the answer is nothing, the diagram may not be needed. If the answer is a distinction, write that distinction in plain sentences next to the image. In the worked example, the distinction was that a site visit can revise the proposal. That single sentence does more work than a longer restatement of the four steps.

Place the copy where it helps. A short lead-in before the diagram can set up what to look for; a short explanation after it can draw out the consequence. Avoid splitting one thought across both positions, which forces the reader to hold half a sentence in mind while looking at a picture.

Keep the language concrete. Name the steps the way your customers would name them, not the way an internal workflow tool labels them. If the diagram says "intake" but customers say "enquiry," align the two.

This is also the place to be careful about claims. A process description is not a promise of speed, availability or outcome. If you want to state something about turnaround, it needs its own evidence and its own wording, separate from the diagram.

## A review brief you can give a web design services team

If you are commissioning or refreshing a service page, a short written brief saves a round of revisions. The following is a template you can adapt; treat the timings as examples, not standards.

Scope. List each diagram on the page and what it is meant to show. One line per diagram is enough.

Caption. Ask for one sentence naming the diagram and its scope, written for a reader who is scanning. Ask the team to flag any caption that makes a comparative claim the diagram cannot support.

Text alternative. Ask for a description of the sequence, including any branch or exception, kept to two or three sentences. Ask them to confirm that decorative images are marked so they are skipped.

Nearby copy. Ask for one sentence before or after the diagram that states the distinction the diagram adds. Ask them to remove any sentence that only repeats the caption.

Consistency. Ask them to check that step names in the diagram, caption, text alternative and body copy match. Mismatched labels are the most common source of confusion in a diagram-heavy page.

Handover. Ask for the caption and text alternative to be stored with the page content, not buried in a design file, so the next editor can find and update them.

The brief does not ask for a redesign; it asks for words that match the picture.

## A plain-language usability check you can run yourself

You do not need analytics to test whether the three layers work. A short manual check catches most problems.

The caption-only test. Cover the diagram and read only the caption. Can you say what the diagram shows? If not, rewrite the caption.

The images-off test. Switch images off in your browser, or read the page in a text-only view. Does the page still make sense? If the diagram's meaning disappears entirely, the text alternative is too thin.

The read-aloud test. Read the text alternative aloud at a normal pace. If it takes more than about twenty seconds, it is probably too long for a four-step diagram. This is a rough heuristic, not a measured standard.

The repetition test. Put the caption, text alternative and nearby copy side by side. If two of the three say the same thing in nearly the same words, cut one.

The distinction test. Ask a colleague who has not seen the page to read the nearby copy and then say what the diagram adds. If they cannot answer, the copy is not doing its job.

The label test. Check that every step name in the diagram appears identically in the text alternative and the body copy. Small differences, such as "site visit" versus "site survey," make readers wonder whether they are the same step.

Run these checks whenever a diagram changes, and again when the surrounding copy is edited, because the two drift apart easily. None of these checks measures traffic or ranking; they measure whether a reader can understand the page, which is the point of the exercise.

## Keeping the three layers aligned over time

A diagram written carefully at launch can quietly fall out of step with the page around it. A service is renamed, a step is removed, a new branch is added, and the caption still describes the old version.

One practical habit is to treat the caption, text alternative and nearby copy as a single unit in your content records. When any one of them changes, review all three. Another is to note, in the page's internal notes, which sentence in the nearby copy carries the diagram's distinction. That sentence is the one to check first when the process changes.

It also helps to decide who owns diagrams. If a designer owns the picture and a writer owns the words, someone needs to own the join between them. A short review brief, like the one above, gives that person something concrete to check against.

Finally, resist the temptation to add diagrams to pages that do not need them. A diagram earns its place when it shows a sequence or a distinction that prose handles poorly. When it does, the caption, text alternative and nearby copy are what turn a picture into something every reader can use.
