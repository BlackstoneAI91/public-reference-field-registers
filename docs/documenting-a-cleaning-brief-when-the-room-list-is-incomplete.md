## Why a room register, not a room list

A room list answers one question: what rooms exist here? A room register answers a harder one: for each room, what has been decided about cleaning scope, who decided it, and who acts next. The difference matters when a venue prepares a commercial cleaning enquiry, because the provider has to read your register and know which rooms are in, which are out, and which are still open.

This reference is written for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers who already have some form of room inventory and want to convert it into a scope record. It covers scope records only. It does not cover cleaning methods, frequencies, rates or equipment choices, and it deliberately uses no real venue information. Every example below belongs to a fictional venue.

One working assumption: a register is only as useful as its weakest row. If a room appears in the register with no decision recorded, the reader cannot tell whether the room was forgotten or intentionally left out. The fields below exist to make that ambiguity impossible to hide.

## The five fields and what each one protects

Stable room identifier. A code that never changes even when the room is renamed, repurposed or split. Something like G-01 or L2-SIDE. The identifier is what the provider quotes back in a quotation, and it is what survives a change of signage. If your identifier is the room name, a rename silently breaks every earlier record.

Public room name. The name people actually use: on signage, in booking systems, in event paperwork. Keep it beside the identifier rather than instead of it. When the two disagree, the identifier wins and the name is annotated.

Scope state. One of three values, defined in the next section. This is the field that turns an inventory into a brief.

Source. Where the decision came from: a written confirmation, a site walk note, a tenancy agreement clause, a verbal instruction later confirmed in writing. The source field is what lets a second reader check the decision without re-running the whole conversation.

Next owner. The single person or role who must act before the row can move. Not a team, not a department. One name or one role, with the action implied by the scope state.

Five fields is enough. Adding more tends to produce columns nobody updates, and an unmaintained column is worse than an absent one because it looks authoritative.

## Three scope states, defined tightly

Confirmed. A decision has been made and recorded, with a source. The room is either in scope or out of scope, and the register says which. A confirmed row needs no further action from anyone until something changes.

Excluded. A deliberate decision that the room is not part of this brief. Exclusion is not the same as absence. An excluded row stays in the register with its source recorded, so that a later reader can see the room was considered and set aside rather than overlooked. Exclusions are frequently the most useful rows in the whole document.

Confirmation needed. The room is known to exist, but the scope decision is unresolved. This state must always carry a next owner, because a row in this state with no owner is a row that will not move.

A useful discipline is to treat any row that does not fit one of these three states as an error, not a fourth category. If a room is half-decided, it is confirmation needed. If a room was decided but the source is missing, it is also confirmation needed until the source is written down.

## One complete sample row

The following row belongs to a fictional venue and is included only to show the fields working together.

Identifier: G-01
Public room name: Main Hall
Scope state: Confirmed (in scope)
Source: Written confirmation from the venue coordinator, dated and filed with the enquiry pack
Next owner: None required until change

Read the row as a sentence: room G-01, known publicly as the Main Hall, is confirmed in scope, the decision is traceable to a written confirmation, and nobody needs to act. A provider reading this row knows exactly what to do with it, which is nothing except include it.

Now compare a second fictional row:

Identifier: L2-SIDE
Public room name: Side Room (formerly Training Room)
Scope state: Confirmation needed
Source: Site walk note; tenancy clause referenced but not yet checked
Next owner: Venue coordinator

This row tells the provider three things at once: the room exists, its name has changed, and the decision is blocked on a specific person checking a specific document. That is a far more useful record than a blank cell.

## Why a row without a decision stays visible

Deleting an undecided row feels tidy and is almost always a mistake. A deleted row removes the only evidence that the room was ever considered. When the enquiry reaches a provider, the room simply does not appear, and the provider has no way to ask about it.

Keeping the row visible with a confirmation needed state does three things. It preserves the question. It names the person who can answer it. And it gives the provider a legitimate reason to raise the point before work is quoted, rather than after.

There is a second reason, closer to how venues actually operate. Rooms change use. A store room becomes a meeting room; a side room gains a second tenant. A register that keeps excluded and undecided rows visible carries that history, so the next person preparing an enquiry starts from a record rather than from memory.

A practical heuristic, offered as an author suggestion rather than a rule: before sending an enquiry, count the rows in each state. If confirmation needed is a large share of the register, the brief is not ready and the next owners should be chased first.

## Where the register meets the provider

The register is the input; the enquiry is the output. A provider working from a clean register can quote against named rooms instead of a general description, and can flag gaps before a site visit rather than during one.

## Blank copyable form

Copy the block below into a document and add one row per room. Keep the column order fixed so that two registers from two venues can be read side by side.

| Identifier | Public room name | Scope state | Source | Next owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| G-01 | Main Hall | Confirmed (in scope) | Written confirmation, filed | None required until change |
| L2-SIDE | Side Room (formerly Training Room) | Confirmation needed | Site walk note; tenancy clause unchecked | Venue coordinator |
| B-04 | Store Room | Excluded | Coordinator instruction, written | None required until change |
| (ID) | (room name) | (scope state) | (source) | (owner) |

Fill rules, in order:

1. Assign the identifier first, before any scope discussion. Never reuse an identifier for a different room.
2. Record the public name exactly as it appears on signage, and note former names in brackets.
3. Choose one of the three states. If unsure, use confirmation needed.
4. Write the source as a document or a dated note, not as a person's memory.
5. Name one next owner for every confirmation needed row, and leave the field empty only when the state is confirmed or excluded.

A short review pass before sending the enquiry: check that no row has a blank state, that every confirmation needed row has an owner, and that every excluded row has a source. Those three checks catch most of the gaps that otherwise surface later in a conversation.

## Keeping the register alive after the first enquiry

A register built for one enquiry tends to be reused for the next one, and that is where it earns its keep. Two habits help.

First, date the register and keep old versions. A scope decision made last year may still be correct, but the reader should be able to see when it was made and against which version of the venue.

Second, treat a change of room use as a trigger to revisit the row, not to delete it. Rename the public name, keep the identifier, and reset the state to confirmation needed with a named owner. The row then carries its own history.
